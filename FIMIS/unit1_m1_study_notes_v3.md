## SECTION 1: THE FINANCIAL SYSTEM – CONCEPT, ROLES & CONSTITUENTS

### 1. Conceptual Meaning and Formal Definitions
* **Finance:** The dynamic process of mobilizing, raising, and allocating funds, and managing the resulting streams of income to achieve specific macro- and micro-economic objectives [516].
* **Financial System:** A complex, well-integrated set of sub-systems of financial institutions, markets, instruments, and services that facilitate the transfer and allocation of funds from surplus units to deficit units in an economy [512, 516].
* **Christy's Theoretical View:** The primary objective of the financial system is to supply funds to different sectors of the economy in ways that promote the fullest and most efficient use of resources—without destabilizing prices or interfering unnecessarily with individual choices [517].
* **Robinson's Theoretical View:** The primary function of the financial system is to act as a crucial link between savings and investment, thereby creating new wealth and permitting adjustments in how existing wealth is held [517].

#### 🔍 How to Elaborate on the Flow of Funds [516, 591]
In a modern economy, the financial system is the primary vehicle for capital accumulation and economic growth [590]. It operates as a matrix linking:
* **Suppliers of Funds (Surplus Units):** Primarily households whose income exceeds their consumption and planned investment ($Y > C + I$) [516, 591].
* **Seekers of Funds (Deficit Units):** Primarily corporate business firms and governments whose consumption and planned investment exceed their current income ($Y < C + I$) [516, 591].
* **The Intermediation Channel:** Funds flow either **directly** via financial markets (issuing primary securities to savers) or **indirectly** through financial intermediaries/institutions (issuing secondary/indirect claims like deposits to savers, and in turn holding primary claims on borrowers) [106, 591].

```
│                        MACRO-ECONOMIC FUND FLOW                        │

         ▼                                                     ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────┐                 ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│  SURPLUS UNITS (Ultimate Lenders)│                 │  DEFICIT UNITS (Ultimate Borrowers)│

```

### 2. Core Characteristics & Structural Features [518]
If a question asks to discuss the features of a financial system, elaborate on these six dimensions:
1. **Bridges Savers & Investors:** Channels stagnant household liquid cash into active, productive industrial capital [518].
2. **Expands Financial Markets:** Fosters the geographical and temporal expansion of financial markets, allowing long-distance and long-term financial commitments [518].
3. **Allocative Efficiency:** Guarantees that scarce capital resources are directed toward projects with the highest productive potential and return profiles, optimizing national GDP [518].
4. **Reduces Transaction and Information Costs:** Lowers search, transaction, and monitoring costs for individual participants by achieving economies of scale in asset management [78, 93].
5. **Drives Credit and Liquidity:** Fosters continuous credit availability to ensure the smooth, uninterrupted functioning of trade and commerce [511].
6. **Systemic Soundness & Regulation:** Governed by stringent regulatory frameworks (RBI, SEBI) that establish contract enforcement, protect investor rights, and limit systemic risks [79, 528].
  * *Regulatory & Coordinating Bodies:* The Indian system features several apex regulators and self-regulatory/coordinating bodies:
    - **Reserve Bank of India (RBI):** Regulates the banking and money markets.
    - **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI):** Regulates the securities and capital markets.
    - **IRDAI & PFRDA:** Regulate insurance and pensions, respectively.
    - **AMFI:** Association of Mutual Funds of India (SRO for mutual funds).
    - **AMBI:** Association of Merchant Bankers of India (coordinating body for merchant banking and primary issues).

### 3. The Four Core Constituents (Interdependence & Details) [519, 528]
The formal financial system is composed of four distinct but continuously interacting and interdependent constituents [519, 528]:

#### A. Financial Institutions (Intermediaries) [520, 582]
These are specialized financial bodies that mobilize public savings and facilitate capital allocation [520].
* **Banking Institutions (Creators of Credit):** Possess the statutory power to accept public demand deposits and actively create secondary deposits (credit) through lending operations [437, 520].
* **Non-Banking Financial Institutions (Purveyors of Credit):** Cannot accept demand deposits or create money, but act as vital purveyors of credit to targeted sectors (e.g., DFIs, NBFCs, Mutual Funds, Insurance) [13, 520].
  * *Development Financial Institutions (DFIs) in India:* Key specialized institutions highlighted in the curriculum include:
    - **IDBI:** Industrial Development Bank of India
    - **ICICI:** Industrial Credit & Investment Corporation of India
    - **IFCI:** Industrial Finance Corporation of India (India's first DFI)
    - **SIDBI:** Small Industries Development Bank of India (MSME focus)
    - **IIBI:** Industrial Investment Bank of India (specifically highlighted as a key historical Indian DFI)
* **🔍 ELABORATION METRIC — The Three Transformation Services:** If asked how financial institutions add value, explain these three services [582]:
  1. **Liability, Asset, and Size Transformation:** They pool thousands of small, granular household deposits to disburse large-sized corporate loans [582].
  2. **Maturity Transformation:** They offer savers liquid, short-term claims (like savings accounts) while simultaneously offering corporate borrowers long-term loans matching project lifecycles [582].
  3. **Risk Transformation:** They mitigate the risk of direct lending for savers by holding highly diversified, professionally managed portfolios, absorbing individual default shocks [582].

#### B. Financial Markets [521, 585]
Mechanisms and platforms that allow participants to deal in financial claims and set prices based on demand and supply [521].
* **Money Market (Short-Term):** Segment for borrowing and lending short-term funds (maturity of less than one year) [521, 583]. Dealt in highly liquid, low-risk debt instruments (e.g. Treasury Bills, Commercial Papers, Certificates of Deposit, Call Money) [521, 583].
* **Capital Market (Long-Term):** Segment for mobilizing long-term funds (maturity of one year or more) in equity and debt instruments [521, 585, 1103].
  * *Primary Market:* The "New Issues Market" where fresh capital is raised by unlisted (IPOs) or listed (FPOs) corporates [521, 667, 674].
  * *Secondary Market:* The stock market (BSE/NSE) where already outstanding or existing securities are traded, providing critical liquidity [521, 585, 664].
* **🔍 ELABORATION METRIC — Interdependence of Primary and Secondary Markets:** If a question asks to analyze the relationship between primary and secondary capital markets, use these points [586]:
  1. **Pricing Baseline:** The secondary market provides the pricing inputs, valuation multiples (P/E), and market expectations necessary to price new issues in the primary market [586].
  2. **Liquidity Incentive:** Investors are only willing to buy fresh, unlisted shares in the primary market because they are assured of an "exit route" via secondary stock exchanges [585].
  3. **Supply-Demand Feedback Loop:** A rampant primary market increases the supply of listed instruments, deepening secondary market volume [586]. Conversely, excessive bunching of mega-issues in the primary market can temporarily drain liquidity and depress prices in the secondary market [586].

#### C. Financial Instruments (Securities) [521, 582, 1103]
These represent contractual claims for the future payment of money, interest, or dividends [1103].
* **Primary (Direct) Securities:** Debt or equity claims directly issued by the ultimate deficit-spending sectors (borrowers) to the ultimate savers (e.g., Equity Shares, Corporate Bonds, Debentures) [582, 1103].
* **Secondary (Indirect) Securities:** Claims issued by financial intermediaries (like banks or mutual funds) against themselves in order to mobilize funds to acquire primary securities (e.g., Bank Deposits, Mutual Fund Units, Life Insurance Policies) [582, 1104].

#### D. Financial Services [522, 578]
Support systems provided by financial intermediaries to simplify, de-risk, and accelerate transactions [522, 578].
* **Fund-Based Services:** Involve the direct transfer of funds where the intermediary acquires or leases assets and earns interest, dividends, or lease rentals (e.g., Equipment Leasing, Hire Purchase, Venture Capital) [20, 522].
* **Fee-Based Services:** Intermediaries render specialized advisory and evaluative expertise, earning commissions, fees, or service charges without putting up their own funds (e.g., Merchant Banking, Credit Rating, Portfolio Advisory) [7, 522].

### 4. Financial Deepening vs. Financial Broadening [580]
* **Financial Deepening:** Refers to the growth of financial assets in relation to the real economy [580]. It is measured as the ratio of financial assets/liquid liabilities to the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) [580]. High deepening indicates that the financial sector is growing faster than real output, reflecting an advanced stage of development.
* **Financial Broadening:** Refers to the structural expansion of the financial system—specifically, an increase in the variety and number of financial instruments, institutions, and participants [580]. It indicates that the system is becoming more inclusive and multi-channel.

#### 🔍 ELABORATION METRIC — Goldsmith's Flow-of-Funds Indicators [597, 598, 599, 600]
Goldsmith constructed four quantitative ratios to measure financial development and capital accumulation [597]:
1. **Finance Ratio (FR):**
   $$	ext{Finance Ratio} = rac{	ext{Total Financial Issues (Primary + Secondary Claims)}}{	ext{National Income}}$$
   *Significance:* It depicts the overall level of financial deepening in the economy [598]. It indicates the rate of financial development in relation to economic growth [598].
2. **Financial Inter-relation Ratio (FIR):**
   $$	ext{Financial Inter-relation Ratio} = rac{	ext{Total Financial Issues}}{	ext{Net Domestic Capital Formation (Real Assets)}}$$
   *Significance:* It reflects the relationship between the financial structure and the real-asset structure of the economy [598]. It shows the participation of the financial system in capital formation [601].
3. **New Issue Ratio (NIR):**
   $$	ext{New Issue Ratio} = rac{	ext{Total Primary Issues}}{	ext{Net Domestic Capital Formation}}$$
   *Significance:* It measures the proportion of primary claims issued directly by non-financial corporate and government sectors to savers to finance real investments [599].
4. **Intermediation Ratio (IR):**
   $$	ext{Intermediation Ratio} = rac{	ext{Total Secondary Issues (by Intermediaries)}}{	ext{Total Primary Issues}}$$
   *Significance:* It measures the importance of financial intermediaries in channelizing resources [599]. A rising IR indicates greater institutionalization of financing [599].

## SECTION 2: STRUCTURAL ANALYSIS & WEAKNESSES OF THE INDIAN SYSTEM

### 1. Financial Dualism: Formal vs. Informal Sectors [61]
* **Financial Dualism:** The parallel coexistence and cooperation of an organized, modern formal sector and an unorganized, traditional informal sector in the same economy [61].

#### The Informal Financial System: Structure, Pros, and Cons [61, 63, 64, 65]
* **Constituents:** Local moneylenders (relatives, landlords, traders, pawnbrokers), indigenous bankers (multani shroffs, gujarati sharafs), and informal savings associations (chit funds, fixed funds, rotating clubs) [62, 63].
* **Advantages (The Theory of Informal Operations) [64]:**
  * *Low Transaction Costs:* No physical documentation, legal drafting, or KYC procedures are required [64].
  * *Minimum Default Risk:* High default protection due to close social tracking, personal relationships, and local peer pressure [64].
  * *Operational Flexibility:* Doorstep delivery, flexible repayment schedules, and 24/7 availability [61, 64].
* **Disadvantages [65]:**
  * *Exorbitant Interest Rates:* Rates can range from 24% to over 100% per annum, causing debt traps [65].
  * *Unregulated & Exploitative:* No consumer protection laws; recovery methods can be aggressive or extra-legal [65].
  * *Lack of Systemic Integration:* Operates in isolation, meaning the central bank cannot transmit monetary policy signals (like interest rate cuts) to this segment [175].

### 2. Structural Weaknesses of the Indian Financial System [524, 525, 526, 527]
If asked to analyze the structural bottlenecks of the Indian financial landscape, elaborate on these points:
* **Lack of Inter-Regulatory Coordination:** Multiple regulators (RBI for banks, SEBI for capital markets, IRDAI for insurance, PFRDA for pensions) often face overlapping jurisdictions, leading to coordination gaps [524, 528].
* **Monopolistic Market Structures:** Giant public sector institutions (such as LIC in life insurance) have historically held massive market share, which can crowd out private players and slow down product innovation [525].
* **Low Financial Inclusion:** Despite bank nationalization, remote rural populations remain underbanked and still rely heavily on informal moneylenders [525].
* **High Non-Performing Assets (NPAs):** Bad corporate loans heavily lock up banks' credit-creating capacity and require expensive government recapitalization [527].
* **Imprudent Corporate Capital Structures:** Over-dependence on long-term debt (term loans) from DFIs in the pre-reform era left Indian corporates with debt-heavy, fragile capital structures [525].
* **Digital and Cyber Risks:** Rapid digitization has exposed the system to sophisticated cyber frauds, data breaches, and FinTech regulatory challenges [526, 527].

## SECTION 3: THE INTERNATIONAL FINANCIAL SYSTEM (IFS) [529]

### 1. Definition and Structural Purpose [529]
The International Financial System is the global network of institutions, markets, instruments, rules, and capital flows that facilitate trade, cross-border investment, and financial transactions across national boundaries [529].

```
                     ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
                     │  INTERNATIONAL FINANCIAL SYSTEM   │
                     └─────────────────┬─────────────────┘
        ┌──────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                              ▼                              ▼
┌───────────────┐              ┌───────────────┐              ┌───────────────┐
│GLOBAL MARKETS │              │ GLOBAL INSTS  │              │ REGULATORY &  │
│ Forex, Money, │              │ IMF, WB, BIS  │              │ FLOWS         │
│  Capital Mkt  │              └───────────────┘              │ Basel, FATF,  │
└───────────────┘                                             │ FDI, Portfolio│
                                                              └───────────────┘
```

### 2. The Four Pillars of the IFS [530, 531, 532]
1. **Global Financial Markets [530]:**
   * *Foreign Exchange (Forex) Market:* Facilitates the conversion and trading of national currencies, establishing exchange rates [530].
   * *International Money Market:* Handles short-term interbank lending (e.g., Eurocurrency flows) [530].
   * *International Capital Market:* Deals in long-term debt (Eurobonds) and equity (ADRs/GDRs), letting multinational firms raise capital globally [530].
2. **International Financial Institutions [531]:**
   * *International Monetary Fund (IMF):* Acts as the global monetary overseer; provides short-to-medium-term financial assistance to countries suffering from Balance-of-Payments crises [531].
   * *World Bank (IBRD & IFC):* Focuses on long-term development; provides financial loans and technical assistance for infrastructure, health, and education projects in developing countries [531].
   * *Bank for International Settlements (BIS):* Serves as the "bank for central banks", fostering global monetary cooperation and hosting the Basel Committee (BCBS) [531].
3. **Regulatory and Supervisory Frameworks [532]:**
   * *Basel Accords:* International regulatory standards (Basel I, II, III) establishing capital adequacy ratios, risk-weighted asset classifications, and liquidity requirements for banks [532].
   * *Financial Action Task Force (FATF):* An intergovernmental body setting global standards to combat money laundering and terrorism financing [532].
4. **International Financial Flows [532]:**
   * *Foreign Direct Investment (FDI):* Stable, long-term capital where overseas investors acquire substantial equity (>10%) and active management control in a domestic enterprise [193, 532].
   * *Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI/FII):* Shorter-term, highly liquid capital invested in secondary stock and debt markets without active management control [532, 533].
   * *Remittances:* Funds transferred by expatriate workers to their home countries, supporting domestic consumption [533].

### 3. Key Challenges of the IFS [533, 534]
* **Global Imbalances:** Severe disparities in trade balances and currency reserves between major exporting nations and debtor nations can cause systemic friction [533].
* **Regulatory Arbitrage:** Financial entities exploit differences in national regulations by shifting operations to tax havens or loosely regulated jurisdictions, creating global instability [534].
* **Currency Volatility:** Extreme fluctuations in exchange rates disrupt global trade planning and increase hedging costs for corporates [534].
* **Equitable Access:** Ensuring developing and underdeveloped nations can access international capital on fair terms without facing debt traps [534].

## SECTION 4: THE 1991 REFORMS & IMPACT OF LIBERALIZATION

### 1. Pre-Reform Indian Financial Landscape (The Baseline) [138, 145]
Before 1991, India operated a closed, statist, highly controlled, and segmented financial system [146]:
* **Administered Interest Rates:** Interest rates for deposits and loans were fixed by the government, leading to financial repression (near-zero or negative real interest rates) [145, 629].
* **License Raj & MRTP Act:** Strict licensing required for setting up or expanding business capacities [426, 475]. Easing MRTP restrictions was forbidden, capping the growth of large private firms [475].
* **High Reserve Pre-emptions:** Banks faced high Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) and Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) mandates [303, 475]. This pre-empted a vast portion of banks' lendable resources to fund government deficits [145].
* **No Price Discovery:** Resource mobilization in the primary capital market was heavily controlled by the Controller of Capital Issues (CCI), which fixed issue prices arbitrarily, preventing market price discovery [146, 669].

### 2. The 1990–91 Balance-of-Payments Crisis (The Triggers) [139, 476, 477]
* **The Root Cause:** Decades of uncompetitive, insular economic strategy and mounting fiscal deficits funded by borrowing for consumption rather than productive investment [139, 476].
* **Key Statistical Markers (1990–91) [476]:**
  * *Fiscal Deficit:* Stood at a dangerous **8.4% of GDP** [476].
  * *Internal Government Debt:* Ballooned to **53% of GDP** [476].
  * *Inflation Rate:* Escalated to around **13%** [476].
  * *Current Account Deficit (CAD):* Reached **3% of GDP** [476].
  * *Foreign Exchange Reserves:* Plummeted to roughly **$1.2 billion** (June 1991), barely sufficient to cover two weeks of essential imports (including oil) [477].
* **The Triggers [477]:**
  1. *The Gulf War:* Iraq's invasion of Kuwait caused global oil prices to spike and cut off remittances from Indian workers in the Gulf [477].
  2. *Credit Downgrades:* International rating agencies downgraded India's sovereign credit rating, freezing short-term credit and causing investor flight [477].
  3. *The Gold Airlift:* To prevent default, India had to airlift 47 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England and 20 tonnes to the Union Bank of Switzerland as collateral for emergency loans [486].

### 3. The LPG Framework (24 July 1991 Epochal Budget) [478]
Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh introduced structural reforms built on three pillars:
* **Liberalization:** Reducing or eliminating government regulations and restrictions [535]. Eased MRTP Act restrictions, abolished industrial licensing for most sectors, and deregulated interest rates [479].
* **Privatization:** Reducing the role of the public sector; commenced disinvestment of minority stakes in PSUs and opened reserved areas (telecom, power, banking, insurance) to private players [480].
* **Globalization:** Integrating the domestic economy with global markets [535]. Rupee current-account convertibility was established, automatic FDI approval routes were introduced, and FIIs were allowed to invest in Indian stock bourses (1992) [480, 481].
* **Immediate Stabilizing Steps:** Immediate 2-step devaluation of the Rupee (~18.5% total) in July 1991, coupled with import compression and spending cuts to curb current account and fiscal deficits [478, 479].

### 4. Dimension-Wise Impact of Liberalization on the Financial System [539]

| Dimension of Impact | Before Liberalization (Pre-1991) [539] | After Liberalization (Post-1991) [539] |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Financial Institutions** | Public-sector dominated, heavily controlled by licensing. Intermediation was rigid with no competitive pricing [661]. | Entry of private & foreign players (e.g., HDFC, ICICI). Aligned with international standards (Narasimham Committee prudential norms, Basel capital adequacy) [433]. |
| **Financial Instruments** | Limited variety; mostly plain equities and physical paper G-Secs [660, 1045]. | Highly diversified (derivatives, commercial papers, mutual funds, hybrid debt). Completely dematerialized (demat) electronic trading [433, 1045]. |
| **The System Overall** | Administered interest rates, closed to foreign capital, strictly bank-centric [661, 1045]. | Market-driven, globally integrated, and multi-channel system [1045]. |
| **Systemic Risks** | Insulated from global financial shocks [1045]. | Prone to global contagion, exchange volatility, and hot money flows [1045]. |

#### 🔍 ELABORATION METRIC — Trade-offs of Liberalization [539]
* **Positive Trade-offs (Operational & Allocative Efficiency):**
  1. *Fosters Competition:* Ending public monopoly drove down intermediation costs and forced banks to adopt modern technology (e.g., ATMs, online banking, electronic clearing) [308, 433, 536].
  2. *Market-Driven Price Discovery:* Repealing the Capital Issues (Control) Act allowed free pricing and book-building, enabling firms to raise equity capital efficiently [146, 151, 669].
  3. *Global Capital Integration:* Eased capital constraints, allowing blue-chip firms to tap international bourses via ADRs/GDRs and cheap External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) [208, 537].
* **Negative Trade-offs (Exposure & Vulnerability):**
  1. *Global Contagion & Volatility:* Integrating with global markets left India vulnerable to global shocks (e.g., 1997 East Asian crisis, 2008 global financial crisis) [53, 539].
  2. *Sudden FII Reversals (Hot Money):* Heavy reliance on portfolio flows can cause abrupt market crashes if foreign capital exits suddenly in risk-off environments [538, 237].
  3. *Unrecognized NPA Crises:* Stringent asset recognition norms exposed vast hidden bad corporate loans, stressing public sector banks [536, 537].
  4. *Economic Inequality:* Services and credit remained concentrated in urban, tech-savvy hubs, risking the financial exclusion of the rural poor [540].
  5. *New, Unfamiliar Risks:* Financial institutions had to build treasury and risk-management capabilities from scratch, exposing them to early operational gaps, administrative errors, and trading losses.
  6. *Mis-selling Concerns:* Deregulation and the entry of private players led to the mis-selling of complex, highly structured retail instruments (such as Unit Linked Insurance Policies / ULIPs) to savers who did not fully comprehend the underlying risk and cost structures.

## SECTION 5: NMIMS M1 PYQ-BASED MODEL QUESTIONS & ANSWERS

### SECTION A: 2-MARK COMPULSORY-STYLE QUESTIONS

#### Q1. Define the term 'financial system' and state its primary role. [512, 516]
**Answer:** The financial system is a well-integrated set of sub-systems of financial institutions, markets, instruments, and services [512]. Its primary role is to act as an intermediary to mobilize surplus savings from the household sector and allocate them efficiently to deficit-spending sectors (corporates and government) for capital formation and economic growth [512, 516, 590].
* **Elaboration Tip for More Marks:** If asked for more marks, draw the Fundamental Fund Flow diagram showing surplus units ($Y > C + I$) and deficit units ($Y < C + I$) interacting via direct and indirect finance [591].

#### Q2. What is 'financial dualism' in the context of the Indian financial landscape? [61]
**Answer:** Financial dualism refers to the parallel coexistence and cooperation of two distinct financial sectors in developing countries [61]:
1. **The Organized (Formal) Sector:** Regulated strictly by apex bodies like the RBI and SEBI, catering to modern urban spheres [61, 62].
2. **The Unorganized (Informal) Sector:** Unregulated, non-institutional, relying on local moneylenders, indigenous bankers, and chit funds in rural spheres [61, 63].
* **Elaboration Tip for More Marks:** Explain that although informal finance has advantages like low transaction costs and doorstep flexibility, its high interest rates (24-100%) and exploitative nature make formal financial deepening a national priority [64, 65].

#### Q3. State the three 'Transformation Services' provided by financial institutions. [582]
**Answer:** Financial institutions resolve the conflicting preferences of savers and borrowers through [582]:
1. **Liability, Asset, and Size Transformation:** Pooling numerous small-size public deposits to disburse large-sized loans to corporate borrowers [582].
2. **Maturity Transformation:** Offering savers liquid, short-term claims (e.g., demand deposits) while granting borrowers long-term project finance [582].
3. **Risk Transformation:** Minimizing individual credit risk for savers by investing in highly diversified corporate portfolios [582].

#### Q4. Distinguish between 'Financial Deepening' and 'Financial Broadening'. [580]
**Answer:** 
* **Financial Deepening:** Refers to an increase in the volume of financial assets as a percentage of GDP, indicating that the size of the financial intermediary sector is growing relative to the real economy [580].
* **Financial Broadening:** Refers to structural expansion—the growth in the number and variety of financial institutions, instruments, and participants in the market [580].

#### Q5. What is the 'Finance Ratio' (FR) as formulated by Goldsmith? State its significance. [598]
**Answer:** The Finance Ratio is a flow-of-funds indicator of financial development, calculated as [598]:
$$	ext{Finance Ratio} = rac{	ext{Total Financial Issues (Primary + Secondary Claims)}}{	ext{National Income}}$$
* **Significance:** It measures the overall rate of financial development and deepening in relation to economic growth, indicating how separated the acts of saving and investment have become in the economy [598, 600].

#### Q6. Differentiate between Primary and Secondary Securities with examples. [582, 1103, 1104]
**Answer:** 
* **Primary (Direct) Securities:** Debt or equity claims directly issued by ultimate borrowers of capital to ultimate savers (e.g., Equity Shares, Corporate Debentures) [1103].
* **Secondary (Indirect) Securities:** Claims issued by financial intermediaries against themselves to mobilize savings from ultimate lenders (e.g., Bank Fixed Deposits, Mutual Fund Units, Life Insurance Policies) [582, 1104].

#### Q7. Name any two positive impacts of financial liberalization on financial instruments in India. [433, 537]
**Answer:** 
1. **Product Diversity:** Allowed the introduction of innovative, risk-sensitive, and customized corporate instruments such as derivatives, commercial papers, and hybrid debt securities [433, 537].
2. **Dematerialization (Demat):** The transition from physical paper share certificates to electronic trading completely eliminated bad deliveries, physical forgery, and settlement delays [433, 1045].

#### Q8. State any two triggers of the 1991 economic crisis in India. [477]
**Answer:** 
1. **The Gulf War (1990–91):** Iraq's invasion of Kuwait spiked global crude oil import prices and cut off remittances from Indian migrant workers in the Gulf, draining forex [477].
2. **Sovereign Credit Rating Downgrade:** International rating agencies downgraded India, freezing short-term international credit and causing a run on reserves, pushing India to the brink of default [477].

#### Q9. What is 'Regulatory Arbitrage' in the International Financial System? [534]
**Answer:** Regulatory arbitrage is a practice where financial institutions and multinational corporations exploit differences, loopholes, or gaps in regulations between different countries [534]. They shift their high-risk or tax-heavy operations to tax havens or loosely regulated jurisdictions, which can destabilize the global financial system [534].

#### Q10. What are 'Ways and Means Advances' (WMA)? Why were they introduced? [170]
**Answer:** WMA is a temporary loan facility provided by the RBI to central and state governments to meet short-term mismatches in cash receipts and payments [170]. They were introduced in April 1997 to replace the automatic monetisation of government deficits via ad hoc 91-day Treasury Bills, thereby limiting unchecked government spending and enhancing RBI's monetary control [170].

### SECTION B: 3-MARK OPTIONAL-STYLE QUESTIONS

#### Q1. Explain the four constituents of the formal financial system in India, highlighting their interdependence. [519, 528]
**Answer:** The formal financial system comprises four interdependent constituents that continuously interact with each other to allocate resources [519, 528]:
1. **Financial Institutions:** Intermediaries like banks, NBFCs, and mutual funds that mobilize household savings and convert them into productive capital [520, 582].
2. **Financial Markets:** Platforms where claims are bought, sold, and priced. Divided into the Money Market (short-term liquidity) and the Capital Market (long-term investment) [521, 583].
3. **Financial Instruments:** Contractual agreements representing future cash-flow claims (direct like equity shares, or indirect like bank deposits) [1103].
4. **Financial Services:** Specialized advisory and funding services (such as merchant banking, credit rating, and leasing) that assist participants in managing risks and navigating complex markets [522, 578].

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                      INTERDEPENDENT SYSTEM WEB                         │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  Financial Institutions  ───(Issue Claims)───►  Financial Instruments
         │                                               ▲
    (Offers Services)                               (Traded In)
         ▼                                               │
  Financial Services     ───(Facilitate)─────►   Financial Markets
```

* **Theoretical Elaboration (How they interact) [579, 582]:** These constituents do not function in isolation. Financial institutions mobilize savings by issuing *instruments* (deposits, insurance policies) [579]. These funds are deployed to buy primary corporate *instruments* (shares, bonds) which are actively priced and traded in financial *markets* [579]. To facilitate these transactions safely, institutions offer specialized *services* (merchant banking, credit rating) [579].

#### Q2. Analyze the 1990–91 Balance-of-Payments crisis in India with key statistical indicators. [476, 477]
**Answer:** 
* **The Structural Context:** Decades of uncompetitive, highly controlled state intervention ("License Raj") led to massive structural deficits [475, 476]. Government borrowing financed consumption rather than productive assets, creating severe macro-economic imbalances [476].
* **Statistical Indicators of the Crisis (1990–91) [476]:**
  * *Foreign Exchange Reserves:* Plummeted to ~$1.2 billion in June 1991, barely sufficient to cover two weeks of essential imports, placing India weeks away from sovereign default [477].
  * *Fiscal Deficit:* Escalated to a dangerous ~8.4% of GDP [476].
  * *Internal Government Debt:* Ballooned to 53% of GDP, up from 35% in 1985–86 [476].
  * *Inflation Rate:* Escalated to around 13% [476].
  * *Current Account Deficit (CAD):* Stood at ~3% of GDP [476].
* **The Triggers [477]:** The Gulf War spiked global oil import prices and cut off migrant remittances [477]. Concurrently, rating agencies downgraded India's sovereign credit rating, freezing short-term international credit [477]. This forced the government to pledge its gold reserves to avoid a default [486].

#### Q3. Compare and contrast a 'Bank-based' financial system with a 'Market-based' financial system. [81, 82, 86]

#### Q5. Discuss the impact of the 1991 financial liberalization on the functioning of Indian financial institutions. [536, 537]
**Answer:** The Narasimham Committee (1991) recommendations fundamentally restructured the operating standards of Indian financial institutions [304, 536]:
* **Positive Impacts [433, 536]:**
  1. **Infusion of Competition:** Granted licenses to new private sector banks (e.g., HDFC, ICICI) and foreign banks, ending decades of public sector monopoly and driving down transaction costs [433, 536].
  2. **Prudential Standards:** Adopted international best practices, including mandatory Capital Adequacy Ratios (aligned with Basel standards), standardized Income Recognition, and strict asset classification/loan-loss provisioning [304, 536].
  3. **Operational Modernization:** Competitive pressure drove computerization, online trading, credit card networks, and customer-centric retail credit [308, 463, 536].
* **Negative Impacts [536, 537]:**
  1. **NPA Stress Exposed:** The new, strict asset recognition rules forced public sector banks to disclose hidden bad corporate loans, revealing severe balance-sheet stress [536, 537].
  2. **Pressure on Weaker PSUs:** Smaller public sector banks struggled to compete with the technology, capital, and agile talent of private and foreign players [537].

#### Q6. Analyze the components of the International Financial System (IFS) and explain their roles. [530, 531, 532]
**Answer:** The IFS facilitates cross-border capital and trade flows and comprises four pillars [530, 531, 532]:
1. **Global Financial Markets:** Includes the Foreign Exchange (Forex) market for currency exchange, the international money market (short-term interbank flows), and the international capital market (Eurobonds and foreign equities) [530].
2. **International Financial Institutions:** Global organizations that monitor and support stability. The IMF assists with Balance-of-Payments crises; the World Bank funds long-term developmental projects; and the BIS fosters central bank cooperation and drafts Basel standards [531, 532].
3. **Regulatory Frameworks:** Global guidelines to ensure systemic integrity, such as the Basel Committee's standards for banking capital/liquidity risks, and the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) guidelines against money laundering [532].
4. **International Capital Flows:** The actual movements of capital across borders via Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), Portfolio Investment, and migrant Remittances [532, 533].
